AI for Ministry

A practical, self-paced training curriculum for Court Street staff — learning to use Claude as a tool that frees us to do the ministry only people can do.

Format Self-paced · 6 hrs total

How to use this course

Before we start, a quick word on how this is organized and what kind of commitment it's going to ask of you. The short version: this is designed to respect your time.

This training takes about six hours total, spread over roughly three weeks of your normal work rhythm. You don't do it in one sitting. You don't need to block a whole day. The material is broken into modules so you can do a piece on Tuesday morning before staff meeting, another piece on Thursday afternoon, and so on.

The structure goes like this:

  1. Core modules (everyone completes). Four modules that every staff member goes through — foundations, our AI policy, prompting basics, and a first hands-on tour of Claude Desktop including Chat, Cowork, and the Microsoft 365 connector. About 2.5 hours.
  2. One role track (pick yours). Five tracks — Pastoral & Teaching, Communications, Children & Youth, Administration, Stewardship & Outreach. You complete the one that matches your role. About 1.5 hours. If you wear multiple hats, pick the one you spend the most time in and skim the others.
  3. Advanced modules (optional but encouraged). How to build a Claude Project for your ministry area, and a practicum where you apply Claude to a real task and report back at staff meeting. About 2 hours.
  4. Reference appendix. A prompt library you'll use long after the course ends. You'll come back to this weekly for the first few months — and that's the point.
A note on pace

If something clicks and you want to keep going, keep going. If something feels heavy, stop and come back tomorrow. The goal isn't to finish — the goal is to leave this course actually using these tools well in your ministry. A rushed completion doesn't serve anyone.

What you'll need

  • A Court Street Claude account — ask the administrator if you don't have one yet.
  • A Microsoft 365 account on our Court Street tenant (the same account you use for Outlook and SharePoint).
  • A Windows or Mac computer. Some exercises are hard on a phone — and the Cowork surface is desktop-only.
  • A real ministry task to practice on. Not a fake scenario. Bring your actual work to the training.
  • A willingness to try things, see them fail, and try again. That's how fluency happens.

What completion looks like

At the end, you'll have signed the Court Street AI Usage Policy, completed your role track's exercises, built at least one reusable prompt for your ministry area, and presented a "time saved" story at a staff meeting. That's the shape of done.

Core Module 1 of 4 ~ 30 minutes

Foundations — Why We're Doing This

Before a single prompt, before a single login, we need to name why Court Street is bringing these tools in at all. Because the "why" shapes the "how" — and if we get the why wrong, we'll use these tools badly no matter how clever the techniques are.

Why AI, and why now

Most of our staff spend a meaningful portion of each week on work that isn't ministry — it's the scaffolding around ministry. Drafting the newsletter. Summarizing the board packet. Turning sermon notes into small group questions. Writing the thank-you letter to the new donor. Compiling the volunteer schedule. Formatting the bulletin.

None of that work is bad. Much of it is essential. But almost none of it is the reason you came to Court Street. You came here to shepherd people, to teach, to reach the community, to steward what God has entrusted — and the scaffolding eats the hours that should go to the shepherding.

AI tools, used well, shift that ratio. They don't replace ministry. They compress scaffolding. A newsletter that took two hours takes forty minutes. A study guide draft that took a Saturday morning takes fifteen minutes of review. The five hours a week that come back are ministry hours.

"Whatever you do, work at it with all your heart, as working for the Lord, not for human masters." Colossians 3:23

The question isn't whether AI is going to be part of the work of churches — it already is, at the churches around us, in the lives of our members, in the culture. The question is whether we use it thoughtfully and on our terms, or whether it gets used on us.

What AI actually is (and isn't)

Language models like Claude are very good at a specific thing: predicting what words come next, given the words so far. That sounds reductive, but it's the honest description. They're trained on vast amounts of text and have become remarkably skilled at producing plausible, helpful, often insightful text in response to what you type.

What this means practically:

  • They are genuinely useful for drafting, summarizing, explaining, brainstorming, translating, comparing, reorganizing, and many other text-based tasks that fill a staff member's week.
  • They sometimes make things up — the industry calls this "hallucination." They can generate a confident-sounding Bible reference that doesn't exist, or a historical fact that's wrong. This isn't rare. This is a feature of how they work, and it's the single most important thing to remember.
  • They have no beliefs, no faith, no pastoral heart, no memory of our congregation, no awareness of Mrs. Henderson's recent loss or the specific way a particular family has been faithful for thirty years. Everything like that comes from you.
  • They are not counselors, pastors, or priests. They do not replace pastoral care, spiritual direction, or the work of the Spirit. They handle text.
A core habit to form now

Every AI output gets reviewed by a human before it goes anywhere. Every sermon reference verified. Every donor letter personalized. Every scripture citation checked. Treat AI output as a draft from a smart intern who's never met our people — useful, often excellent, but never the final word.

Our tool: Claude Desktop, two surfaces

Court Street has chosen Claude as our AI platform. We use it through the Claude Desktop app — a free application you'll install on your work computer — rather than the website. Desktop gives you faster access, works with your local files, and includes a feature called Cowork that the web version doesn't.

Inside Claude Desktop, you'll use two different surfaces depending on the task. They sit side-by-side as tabs at the top of the window (a third tab, Code, is for developers — ignore it). Most of your work will be in Chat. Some of it will move to Cowork when you want Claude to actually produce files or work across several of them at once.

A simple rule of thumb: if the task ends with "and then I read it and decide what to do," open Chat. If the task ends with "and then a finished file gets saved where I need it," open Cowork. Module 4 walks through installing the app and using both surfaces.

Both surfaces connect to our Microsoft 365 — Outlook, OneDrive, SharePoint, Teams — through a feature called the M365 connector. Once it's set up, you can ask Claude things like "summarize the email thread about the building repair" or "find last year's picnic bulletin in SharePoint" without having to download and re-upload anything. We'll set that up in Module 4 as well.

A theological word before we go further

We are a church. It would be dishonest to pretend that using AI tools is spiritually neutral, that it raises no questions worth thinking about. It does. Let's name a few, briefly, and say where we land.

Does using AI to draft ministry content make it less authentic? No more than using a word processor, a concordance, a commentary, or a sermon illustration book made ministry less authentic. Every pastor in history has drawn from tools and other voices; what matters is that the final work is shaped by your discernment, your voice, your care for the people you serve. AI doesn't change that equation — it just changes the tool.

Is it honest to use AI-assisted writing? In most cases, yes, same as with any editing or co-writing. Where it would matter — a personal letter to a grieving family, a pastoral counseling note, a message where the recipient has a right to your unmediated voice — don't use AI, or use it only as a brainstorming partner and write the piece yourself. Use judgment.

Does AI threaten our call? Only if we let it. The work only people can do — being present in a hospital room, praying with someone in crisis, knowing a child's name, discerning what God is doing in a family's life, carrying the weight of a community — cannot be done by a machine. If AI frees us from drudgery to do more of that work, it serves the call. If we use it lazily, it erodes us. That's on us.

The posture we're taking

AI is a tool, like a typewriter or a photocopier or a study Bible was in its day. It does not pray. It does not love its neighbor. It cannot shepherd a flock. Our calling doesn't change. What changes is that the paperwork that used to steal hours from the calling starts giving those hours back.

Before moving on, check yourself
  • I can name in one sentence why Court Street is adopting these tools.
  • I understand that AI can produce confident-sounding text that is simply wrong, and that I must verify.
  • I know the rough split: Chat for drafting things I'll review, Cowork for tasks that end with a finished file.
  • I'm comfortable that this is a tool, not a replacement for what only people can do.
Core Module 2 of 4 ~ 25 minutes

Our AI Usage Policy

Every Court Street staff member signs this policy before using Claude for church work. It's short on purpose. The heart of it is two lists: things we never do, and things we always do.

The Never list

These are absolute. If a situation pushes against any of these, stop, close the Claude window, and either handle the task without AI or talk to a pastor or admin about what approach is appropriate.

  • Never paste counseling notes, mental-health conversations, or pastoral crisis content into Claude. These contain the deepest trust a person places in the church. They do not go into any external tool, ever, under any circumstance. This applies in both Chat and Cowork.
  • Never paste a child's full name, photo, or identifying details combined with church affiliation. Children's ministry work uses first names only, or generic descriptions, in any AI prompt. Photos do not go in.
  • Never paste identifiable giving records, donor names tied to amounts, or individual financial data. Aggregate and anonymized data is fine. Named individual donor data stays in our approved financial systems.
  • Never paste Social Security numbers, full birthdates, home addresses of minors, login credentials, or any similar high-sensitivity personal data. If you'd be uncomfortable with it on a postcard, it doesn't go in AI.
  • Never publish AI-generated content as the Pastor's own words (or any staff member's) without their review and explicit approval.
  • Never rely on AI for doctrinal or scriptural accuracy without verification. Claude generates plausible-sounding theology that is sometimes wrong. Scripture references get checked against an actual Bible. Doctrinal statements get reviewed by a pastor.
  • Never use AI to respond to a person in crisis. Crisis calls, mental health emergencies, pastoral emergencies — these are never handled through AI-assisted responses. A human being, present and aware, responds.

The Always list

  • Always review before you send, post, or publish. Read every AI output as if it came from an intern who's never met our people. Fix, personalize, verify.
  • Always verify scripture and facts. Any Bible reference gets checked. Any historical claim gets sanity-checked. Any statistic gets a source.
  • Always sign in with your Court Street Claude account. Use the account set up for you by the administrator, not a personal account. This keeps church work inside our organization's settings and audit trail.
  • Always use generic references for sensitive scenarios. Instead of "write a note for Jane Smith whose husband just died of cancer," write "help me draft a condolence letter for a longtime member whose spouse recently passed." The specific name and details go in at the end, in your own hand.
  • Always assume the conversation could be read aloud in a staff meeting. If that's uncomfortable, rework the prompt.
  • Always ask if you're not sure. The admin and the pastor can help think through whether a specific use is appropriate. It's better to ask than to guess.
A specific note about Cowork

Cowork stores its conversation history locally on your work computer rather than in Claude's cloud. That's actually more private in one sense — it stays on your machine — but it also means Cowork conversations are not captured in our organization's audit logs. For the church, this has two practical consequences:

  • Cowork is fine for ordinary ministry work — drafting, file organization, producing documents from non-sensitive sources.
  • For anything touching the Never list — and especially anything pastoral or financial — use Chat, not Cowork. Chat conversations are visible to the administrator if there's ever a reason to review them; Cowork sessions are not.
Signing the policy

Before using Claude for Court Street work, sign the one-page AI Usage Policy (on file with the church administrator). This is a one-time thing and takes thirty seconds. It isn't a legal formality — it's a shared statement that we've all read the Never list and agree to work within it.

What to do if something goes wrong

If you accidentally paste sensitive content into Claude, don't panic — but don't hide it either. Contact the church administrator the same day. We'll walk through what was pasted, take any cleanup steps available (deleting the conversation, clearing local Cowork history, etc.), and document what happened. The reason we handle this openly is that quiet mistakes compound. Early transparency keeps everyone safer.

Before moving on
  • I've read both lists carefully and understand why each item is on it.
  • I've signed the one-page policy with the admin.
  • I know who to contact (the administrator) if I'm uncertain whether a use is appropriate.
  • I understand that errors get reported openly, not hidden.
Core Module 3 of 4 ~ 40 minutes

Prompting Basics

A prompt is simply what you type to the AI. Getting good output is about writing good prompts — and that skill is learnable in an afternoon. This module gives you a simple framework and shows you, with real examples, how a mediocre prompt becomes a great one.

The C·R·A·F·T framework

When a prompt feels like it's getting mediocre output, it's almost always because one of these five pieces is missing. Run through them as a mental checklist.

C
Context
The background the model needs to do the job
R
Role
Who the AI is acting as
A
Audience
Who the output is for
F
Format
Shape of the output
T
Tone
The feel of the writing

Watch what happens when we stack these.

A bad prompt

Example · WeakClaude
Write a newsletter for church.
Output will be generic, flavorless, and unusable. The model has no idea what church, what audience, what tone, what length, or what content to include.

A better prompt

Example · BetterClaude
Write a short weekly newsletter for Court Street Christian Church in Salem, Oregon. Highlight Sunday service, the upcoming church picnic on March 22, and this week's volunteer opportunity (greeting team needs two people).
Usable. Output will be on-topic. But still somewhat generic in voice, and you'll likely get more polish by pushing further.

A great prompt

Example · StrongClaude
You are a communications writer helping Court Street Christian Church, a warm, scripture-grounded congregation in Salem, Oregon. Our tagline is "Loving God. Loving Neighbor. Together." Draft this week's midweek email newsletter, about 250 words, for our regular members (mostly 35–70 years old, many with families). Warm, conversational tone — like a friend writing an update. Use second person. Include: • A brief opener tied to this Sunday's sermon theme (forgiveness, Matthew 18) • A highlight of the March 22 all-church picnic at Bush Pasture Park (12 PM, potluck, RSVP in the app) • A short invitation for two volunteers on the greeting team this Sunday • A closing line of encouragement Break into short paragraphs. End with "See you Sunday," — Pastor [Name]. Don't invent scripture quotes I didn't give you; if you reference Matthew 18, reference it briefly and accurately.
All five CRAFT elements are present. The output will be 90% of the way to ready-to-send. You'll spend five minutes editing instead of forty minutes writing.

The four habits of good prompters

  1. Be specific about who the AI is and who it's writing for. "You are helping a children's ministry director write a note to parents of K–2 kids" produces radically different output than "write a note."
  2. Give examples whenever possible. If you have a newsletter you liked, paste it in and say "match this voice." If you have a condolence letter you've sent before, paste it in and say "new letter, same spirit." Models learn your style from samples much faster than from descriptions.
  3. Iterate — don't settle for the first draft. The first output is a starting point. Say "make it warmer," "shorter," "remove the second paragraph," "add a reference to Psalm 23," "rewrite for a teenager." Keep going until it's right. This is the skill.
  4. Tell it what not to do, not just what to do. "Don't use the word 'journey,'" "no hashtags," "don't invent scripture references," "don't be saccharine." Negative instructions prevent the AI clichés you want to avoid.
Try This · 10 minutes
Rewrite a prompt, three times

Open Claude. Pick a real task from your inbox this week — something you'd normally write from scratch. Try a bad prompt, a better prompt, and a great prompt using CRAFT. Keep the conversations. Notice the difference.

Bonus: take your "great" version and iterate three more times until the output is ready to send. Count how long it took compared to writing from scratch.

Verifying output — the three checks

Before anything the AI produced leaves your screen for the congregation, run three checks. Make this a reflex, like looking both ways at a crosswalk.

  1. The accuracy check. Is every scripture reference real? Is every fact right? Is every name spelled correctly? Are any claims about history, doctrine, or statistics actually true?
  2. The voice check. Does this sound like Court Street? Does it sound like you? Not like an AI wrote it? If it has a vaguely corporate, "journey together" quality, rework it.
  3. The pastoral check. Is this the right thing to say to this audience, at this moment, given what I know about our people? Would I be comfortable with our most thoughtful member reading this?
Watch out for: Confident-sounding nonsense

The single failure mode you will hit most often is a beautifully written output that contains a fabricated Bible reference, a wrong date, or an invented quote attributed to a theologian. AI writes these confidently. They look right. Always verify specifics.

Before moving on
  • I can name the five letters of CRAFT and what each one means.
  • I've rewritten a real prompt three times and seen the difference in output.
  • I have the three-check reflex: accuracy, voice, pastoral.
  • I know iterating in conversation is where real quality comes from.
Core Module 4 of 4 ~ 60 minutes

Claude Desktop 101

This module is hands-on. You'll install the Claude Desktop app, sign in, connect it to Microsoft 365, and try a small task in both Chat and Cowork. Don't skip the exercises — fluency comes from doing, not reading. Plan on about an hour, though most of that time is the install and M365 admin consent.

Step 1 — Install the Claude Desktop app

We use the desktop app, not the website. It's faster, it works with your local files, and it includes the Cowork surface that the web version doesn't. Installation takes about three minutes.

  1. Go to claude.com/download in your browser.
  2. Click the download button for your computer (Windows or Mac — the site auto-detects).
  3. Open the installer when it finishes downloading. On Windows that's a .msix file; on Mac it's a .pkg. Follow the prompts — there's nothing unusual to click through.
  4. Launch Claude from your Start menu (Windows) or Applications folder (Mac). You can pin it to your taskbar or dock.
  5. Sign in with the Claude account the administrator set up for you. If you don't have one yet, ask the admin before going further — don't create a personal account for church work.
If you get stuck

The official install guide is at support.claude.com. If Windows flags the installer as unfamiliar, that's normal — click "More info" and "Run anyway." If you run into anything beyond that, ping the administrator rather than fighting it for thirty minutes.

Step 2 — The three tabs you'll see

When Claude Desktop opens, you'll see three tabs at the top of the window: Chat, Cowork, and Code. Here's what each one does and which ones you'll use.

  • Chat is the conversation surface. It looks and works like the Claude website — you type, Claude answers, you keep going. This is where most of your work will happen.
  • Cowork is the agent surface. Instead of chatting, you point Claude at a folder on your computer and give it a task to complete. Claude works through the task on its own and saves finished files where you told it to. We'll cover this in Step 5.
  • Code is for software developers. Ignore it. It's not for church work, and clicking it by accident does nothing harmful.

Step 3 — Connect Microsoft 365

The M365 connector lets Claude read (not modify) your Outlook email, OneDrive and SharePoint documents, Teams conversations, and calendar. That means instead of downloading a document from SharePoint and uploading it to Claude, you can just ask Claude to find and summarize it directly. Same with email threads and meeting notes.

The connector is read-only and respects your existing Microsoft 365 permissions — Claude can't see anything in Microsoft 365 that you couldn't already see yourself. It uses your login, not a separate set of keys. The security guide has the details if you're curious.

One-time admin step

Before any individual staff member can connect, a Microsoft 365 Global Administrator at Court Street has to grant tenant-wide consent. The administrator handles this. If you try to connect before that's done, you'll get an authentication error — that just means the admin step is still pending, not that you did anything wrong.

Once the admin has granted consent, here's how to connect your own account:

  1. In Claude Desktop, click your profile icon in the bottom-left corner, then choose Settings.
  2. Go to Connectors (on some versions it's under Customize → Connectors).
  3. Find Microsoft 365 in the list and click Connect.
  4. A Microsoft sign-in window opens. Sign in with your Court Street Microsoft 365 account (not a personal @outlook.com — those don't work).
  5. Review the permissions Claude is asking for (read access to SharePoint, OneDrive, Outlook, Teams) and accept.
  6. You'll bounce back to Claude Desktop with the connector marked Connected. That's it.

The full official walkthrough is at Enable and use the Microsoft 365 connector.

Step 4 — Using Chat

The conversation itself

Click the Chat tab. You're looking at a message box. Type, press enter, Claude answers. Keep the conversation going — don't start a new chat every time you want to refine something. Claude remembers everything in the current conversation and uses that context.

File uploads

Click the paperclip. Upload a PDF, Word doc, Excel sheet, text file, or image. Claude reads the entire thing (even full books) and can answer questions, summarize, pull out sections, rewrite, or compare against another document. This is one of Chat's biggest strengths.

The Microsoft 365 connector in action

With the connector on, you can skip the upload step for anything that's already in Microsoft 365. Just ask: "Find the 2024 building maintenance report in our SharePoint and summarize it." Or: "Summarize the email thread with the Salem community garden about Saturday's work day." Claude will pull the document or thread and work with it directly. You'll see a small indicator telling you which files or messages it looked at.

Projects

A Project is a persistent workspace where Claude remembers things across many conversations. You upload reference documents once (style guide, doctrinal statement, past sermons, newsletter archive), and every conversation inside that Project automatically has that context. Module 5 walks through setting up the five Court Street Projects.

Artifacts (creating files)

Ask Claude to "create this as a Word document I can download" or "make this a printable PDF" and it will produce a downloadable file right in the chat. For emails, bulletins, study guides, and proposals — you can skip the copy-paste-into-Word step entirely.

Try This · 15 minutes · Chat
Three small tasks, three Chat features

In Claude Desktop, click the Chat tab. Do all three of these as one continuous conversation to build muscle memory.

  • Task 1 — Pure chat. Type: "I'm a staff member at Court Street Christian Church, a congregation in Salem, Oregon. Help me brainstorm five creative, scripture-grounded ideas for our Easter breakfast theme this year." Then keep refining: "The first idea feels too cheesy — try three different ones," and so on.
  • Task 2 — M365 connector. Ask: "Search our SharePoint for anything related to last year's Easter breakfast and summarize what we did." (If the connector hasn't been set up yet, use file upload instead — upload any recent bulletin and ask for a summary.)
  • Task 3 — Artifact. Ask Claude to "create a one-page volunteer signup sheet for the Easter breakfast I can print, as a PDF." You'll get a downloadable file. Save it.

Step 5 — Using Cowork

Cowork is a different way of working with Claude. Instead of a conversation where you copy output out, Cowork is a worker: you hand it a task, it works through the steps on its own, and you come back to a finished file. Under the hood it has the same brain as Chat, but it can read and write files on your computer, run multi-step tasks autonomously, and produce real Word documents, Excel spreadsheets, PDFs, and PowerPoint decks saved directly to a folder you chose.

Why Cowork is worth learning

Chat is brilliant for anything that ends with "and now I'll read and edit this draft." Cowork is brilliant for anything that ends with "and now three finished files are sitting in the right folder." A staff member learning both has roughly twice as many workflows available as someone using only Chat.

Opening Cowork for the first time

  1. Click the Cowork tab at the top of Claude Desktop.
  2. At the bottom of the screen you'll see a message box and a Work in a Folder checkbox. Click it and choose a folder on your computer. Start with something non-sensitive — a folder of old bulletins, an empty folder you'll use for practice — not your whole Documents drive.
  3. Type a task. "In this folder, there are three old bulletins as PDFs. Read them, identify the three most common announcement types, and create a Word document template for a weekly bulletin based on that pattern. Save it in this folder."
  4. Hit send and walk away. Claude narrates what it's doing as it works. When it's done, the file is in the folder.

What Cowork is good at

  • Producing finished files — Excel spreadsheets with working formulas, formatted Word documents, PowerPoint decks, filled-in PDFs.
  • Working across many files at once — "read all twelve of these meeting minutes from the last year, extract every action item, and produce a single consolidated spreadsheet."
  • Organizing and reorganizing — "sort this folder of photos by year based on the dates in the filenames and move them into subfolders."
  • Recurring scheduled work — set Cowork to run a task weekly or monthly (more on this once you're comfortable with the basics).

What Cowork is NOT for, at Court Street

  • Anything on the Never list. Pastoral, counseling, child-safety, or donor-specific content.
  • Work that someone else on staff needs to see the conversation for. Cowork sessions are stored on your local computer and aren't visible to the administrator.
  • Quick back-and-forth drafting. If you're going to review the output anyway, Chat is faster.
Try This · 15 minutes · Cowork
Your first Cowork task

Create an empty folder on your desktop called claude-practice. Drop in any three or four non-sensitive church documents (old bulletins, past newsletters, a sample sermon outline). Open Cowork, click Work in a Folder, and choose that folder.

  • Task: "Read each of the documents in this folder. Produce a single-page Word document called voice-notes.docx that summarizes the tone, vocabulary patterns, and typical length of Court Street's written voice. I'll use it as a reference for the newsletter."
  • Watch it work. It will open each file, read it, take notes, and produce the doc.
  • When it's done, open voice-notes.docx. How useful is what it produced? What would you change about the prompt to get a better result next time?

Pastoral & Teaching

Preaching, teaching, and pastoral care are the most voice-sensitive work the church does. AI can be a real help here — but only if you hold it at the right distance. This track focuses on the uses that genuinely free you to do more ministry, and names the lines we don't cross.

The governing principle

AI is a research assistant and a first-draft partner — never a ghostwriter for what you're going to say from the pulpit or in a pastoral moment. The pastor's voice stays the pastor's voice. Anything going out under your name should be recognizably you to someone who knows you.

Use case 1 · Sermon research

Where AI shines is in the preparation stage — gathering background, identifying commentators' positions, surfacing cross-references, suggesting angles you hadn't considered. Think of it as a well-read research assistant at your side, not as a writer.

Sermon research · Background & anglesClaude
I'm preparing a sermon on Luke 15:11-32 — the prodigal son — for Sunday, April 27. Our congregation is Court Street Christian Church in Salem, Oregon — mixed ages, mostly long-time members with some younger families. The sermon series we're in focuses on "Grace in Unlikely Places." Help me by providing: 1. Three different interpretive angles scholars and preachers have taken on this passage beyond the most common "God the loving father" reading. Give me the less-traveled paths. 2. The historical/cultural context a modern listener would miss — what did inheritance, feasting, and the father running mean in that era? 3. Five potential cross-references that would deepen the text without overwhelming it. 4. Three questions the passage might press on a comfortable suburban congregation that we shouldn't flinch from. Don't write the sermon. Just give me material to work from.
Notice the final instruction. This prevents Claude from drafting a sermon. You stay the author; Claude stays the assistant.

Use case 2 · Small group study guides

Converting a sermon into a small group discussion guide used to take a whole Saturday. It can now take twenty minutes, and the guide is often better for it — because you have the bandwidth to actually review and tailor it.

Sermon → study guideClaude
I preached the attached sermon on Sunday (full manuscript attached). Turn it into a 45-minute small group discussion guide with these sections: • OPENING (5 min): A warm-up question tied to the sermon's theme that everyone can answer in one sentence. • SCRIPTURE (10 min): The main passage read together, plus 2 observation questions ("what do you notice?") before any interpretation. • DISCUSSION (20 min): 4 discussion questions that move from the text → to meaning → to personal application. Use open-ended questions, not yes/no. • APPLICATION (5 min): One practical "this week, try..." invitation. • PRAYER (5 min): A brief prayer prompt connected to the theme. Keep the voice warm and accessible. Our groups are mixed in biblical knowledge — don't assume seminary vocabulary. Don't invent scripture references I didn't use.

Use case 3 · Pastoral letters

For the many routine pastoral communications — membership welcome, new-baby congratulations, get-well notes, membership transfer letters — AI drafts a strong starting point, and you personalize. For the weightier letters — bereavement, crisis, conflict — handle differently (see below).

Pastoral letter · New member welcomeClaude
Draft a personal welcome letter from me (the senior pastor) to a new member at Court Street Christian Church. The letter should: • Be warm and specific, not generic • Be short — 4 short paragraphs max • Acknowledge that joining a church is a significant step • Name two or three ways they can connect at Court Street (a weekly small group, the hospitality team, Sunday hospitality hour) • Offer a concrete invitation — a coffee meeting in the next month if they'd like • End with a specific prayer, not a generic one Write in a voice that is warm but not saccharine, scripture-informed but not preachy. Leave [NAME] and [SPECIFIC DETAIL THEY SHARED] as placeholders I'll fill in. Sign-off: "With joy, Pastor [Name]"
For bereavement and crisis letters

Do not use AI to draft letters for deaths, serious illness, family crisis, or estrangement. These letters carry the pastor's presence into a moment when the recipient needs you, not a polished paragraph. If you want to brainstorm, use AI to think through approach (what themes, what to avoid) — but write the letter yourself.

Use case 4 · Counseling preparation

Before a counseling appointment on a subject you haven't worked with recently — grief in adolescence, addiction recovery, infertility, faith deconstruction — AI can surface what good pastoral care in that area looks like: common pitfalls, helpful frameworks, questions that open rather than close.

Name rules

Never type the person's name or identifying details. Use "a person in their 40s experiencing X" or "a parent whose teenager is Y." Keep the prompt fully anonymized. The purpose is general preparation, not case-specific advice.

Counseling prep · Background readingClaude
I'm meeting with a parent whose adult child is going through a faith deconstruction — walking away from the Christian faith they were raised in. The parent is heartbroken and looking for pastoral support. Before the conversation, help me think through: 1. What does sensitive, non-anxious pastoral care look like for this parent? What are the traps to avoid? 2. What is generally unhelpful to say to a parent in this moment? (I want to know what NOT to do.) 3. What good questions might I ask that help the parent feel heard, rather than lectured or fixed? 4. Are there any scripture passages or pastoral frameworks that have been genuinely helpful for people in this exact situation — not prooftexts, but deep ones? I'm not looking for a script. I'm looking for a better posture walking in.

Use case 5 · Reading a long document fast

Board packets. Annual reports. Denominational policy documents. Theology books. Claude can read a 60-page document in seconds and let you ask questions about it — with citations back to the page.

Are there any sections where the author's reasoning feels weak or assumes something that isn't argued for? 3. What would a critical reader push back on? Cite page numbers for anything you quote.

Communications & Marketing

The communications role at a church is a firehose: newsletters, social posts, bulletins, email campaigns, event promotion, announcement scripts, website copy, volunteer appeals. This is the role where AI gives the most time back to the most people — if voice consistency is held.

The governing principle

Court Street's communications voice is warm, grounded, and slightly understated — we don't oversell, we don't use hype, we don't "journey together." Every piece of AI-drafted comm gets reviewed against that voice. The prompts below include voice guardrails so you don't have to re-litigate this every time.

Track C ~ 75 minutes

Children & Youth Ministry

Children's and youth ministry runs on a thousand small pieces: weekly lesson plans, discussion questions, parent communications, volunteer training, permission slips, event sheets. AI can take much of the preparation load off so you spend more time actually with kids and families.

Child-safety first

Never paste a child's full name, photo, or identifying details into AI. Never. Use generic references ("a third-grader," "a middle school girl"). For any communication that goes to parents, the pastoral review is always by a staff member who knows the family. AI drafts; humans send.

Also include: • The ONE main take-home idea, in one sentence a kid could repeat. • One thing to tell parents so they can reinforce it at home. Stay theologically grounded. Don't invent details that aren't in the passage. Don't dumb down to the point of distortion — respect kids' intelligence.

Use case 5 · A Cowork curriculum library

Advanced Module 1 ~ 45 minutes

Claude Projects — building systems, not one-offs

Everything up to this point has been single-session work: you type a prompt, you get a reply, you move on. Claude Projects change the shape of that. A Project is a persistent shared workspace where context lives across conversations and across team members. This is the single biggest time-saver in the tool.

What a Project is

A Project is a named workspace in Claude that stores:

  • Reference documents — uploads that the AI always has access to (your style guide, past sermons, doctrinal statement, policy documents, curriculum, brand book)
  • Custom instructions — a system prompt that applies to every conversation in the Project ("you are assisting Court Street's children's ministry — voice rules: warm, age-appropriate, never invent scripture, always reference the attached curriculum")
  • Team access — other staff members you've invited can use the same Project, with the same context
  • Conversation history — every chat within the Project stays organized under that Project's name

Once a Project is set up well, every conversation inside it automatically carries the voice, the policies, and the reference material of that ministry area. You no longer have to re-explain "we're a Christian Church in Salem, Oregon, voice is warm and understated..." at the start of every prompt. That's baked in.

Advanced Module 2 ~ 2 weeks

Your practicum — one real task, start to finish

The final module isn't a lesson. It's a practicum. Over the next two weeks, you'll pick one recurring task from your work, redesign it around AI, and present a short "time saved, quality changed" story at a staff meeting. This is where training becomes habit.

Step 1 · Pick your task

Choose a task that meets all three criteria:

  • Recurring. Something you'll do at least monthly, ideally weekly. One-off tasks don't compound.
  • Text-heavy. Something where writing, summarizing, analyzing, or drafting is the main work. Not something that's mostly phone calls or in-person.
  • Currently a time drag. Something that consistently takes longer than you wish it did.

Examples: the weekly newsletter. Sunday school lesson prep. Monthly board report. Meeting minutes. The ongoing grant proposal backlog. The stewardship thank-you list. The curriculum Q&A volunteers keep asking about.

Step 2 · Redesign the task around AI

  1. Document the current workflow. Before you change anything, write down the current steps and roughly how long each takes. Be honest. You'll need this for the comparison.
  2. Design the AI-assisted version. Where does AI fit? What prompt(s) will you use? What still needs a human? What gets reviewed and by whom? Sketch the new workflow.
  3. Build any Project or prompt library you need. If this is the weekly newsletter, build the saved prompt in your Communications Project. If it's lesson prep, build a starter prompt template.
  4. Run the new workflow twice. Not once. Twice. First run will have friction. Second run will reveal whether it actually scales.
  5. Measure. Time, quality, review required. Be honest.

Step 3 · Present at staff meeting

At the staff meeting after your practicum, take three to five minutes to share:

  • The task you worked on
  • The old workflow and time it took
  • The new workflow and time it takes
  • One thing you learned you didn't expect
  • One thing that didn't work — what AI was bad at, or where quality suffered and you had to adjust

Honest reports, including what didn't work, are more valuable than polished success stories. Other staff learn as much from watching you hit a wall as from watching you succeed.

Why this step matters

Every staff member's practicum becomes shared institutional knowledge. By the end of three months, Court Street has five to ten documented workflows that work, plus the lessons from what didn't. That's better than any vendor's training program. It's ours.

Appendix A ~ 10 minutes to read

Appendix A — Dispatch

A footnote for the curious. Dispatch is a new Cowork feature that lets you hand tasks to Claude from your phone while your desk computer does the work. It's useful, it's rough, and it's worth knowing about — but it's not required for any of the workflows in this curriculum.

What Dispatch actually is

Dispatch isn't a separate app. It's a feature inside Cowork that creates one continuous conversation that runs on both your Claude mobile app and your Claude Desktop app. You send Claude a message from your phone on the way to a hospital visit; Claude does the work back at your office computer; the finished file is waiting when you get back. You can send another message from your phone while you're there; Claude keeps going.

This is different from the regular mobile app, where Claude runs in the cloud and can't touch your files. With Dispatch, your desktop machine is the worker — same folders, same M365 connector, same Cowork skills. Your phone is just the remote control.

Availability

Dispatch is currently a research preview for Claude Pro and Claude Max plan users. If the Court Street Claude account is on a plan that includes it, you'll see a Dispatch option in the Cowork sidebar. If you don't see it yet, the feature hasn't reached your tier — just wait; Anthropic has been rolling it out broadly.

When a church staff member might want this

Most Court Street work doesn't need Dispatch. Chat on the mobile app is already great for quick drafting on the go. But a few scenarios genuinely benefit:

  • You're driving back from a hospital visit and want to send a task that will pull a care-team update from SharePoint and draft a summary for the pastor — ready by the time you walk in.
  • You're at a community event and realize the board packet needs one more section. Dispatch it from your phone; it's done before you get to your desk.
  • A recurring Friday task (pulling metrics, generating a digest) where you'd rather kick it off while you're waking up, not after you've settled in.

Setting it up

  1. Update both apps. Make sure Claude Desktop on your work computer and the Claude mobile app on your phone are both on the latest version. Download the mobile app from the App Store or Google Play if you haven't.
  2. Pair them. In Claude Desktop, click the Cowork tab, then find Dispatch in the sidebar. Click Get started. A QR code appears.
  3. Scan the code. Open the Claude mobile app and tap the Dispatch entry. Point your phone at the QR code on your desktop screen. That's it — no passwords, no extra setup.
  4. Turn on keep-awake. During setup you'll see a toggle that prevents your computer from going to sleep while Dispatch is running. Turn it on, or your tasks will stall the moment the lid closes.

The official walkthrough, which Anthropic keeps updated, is at support.claude.com · Assign tasks to Claude from anywhere in Cowork.

What to know before you rely on it

  • Your work computer must stay awake and signed in with Claude Desktop open. If the computer sleeps, the task pauses.
  • One continuous thread. You can't start a new Dispatch conversation — everything is one long, running chat. Claude remembers prior tasks in the same thread, which is often helpful and occasionally a problem.
  • No task-complete notifications yet. Claude doesn't ping you when the work is done. You have to check back.
  • It's still rough. Expect maybe half of tasks to complete cleanly on the first try, especially anything involving screen interactions. It will improve.
  • All the church policy still applies. The Never list is the Never list, whether the prompt came from your phone or your desktop. Dispatch sessions are stored locally on your work computer just like regular Cowork, so the same audit-log caveat from Module 2 applies — pastoral, financial, and child-safety content doesn't get dispatched.
A specific security note

Dispatch gives your phone the ability to trigger real actions on your office computer — reading files, using the Microsoft 365 connector, clicking through apps. If your phone is ever lost or unattended at a meeting, someone with it could send tasks that run on your desk machine. Use a phone lock screen. If you lose the device, unpair it immediately from Claude Desktop's Dispatch settings.

That's the footnote. If Dispatch sounds useful for your rhythm, set it up; if it sounds like more friction than it's worth, skip it. The course works either way.

Appendix B — Scheduled Tasks

A second footnote, and the more useful of the two for most church staff. Scheduled Tasks let you write a prompt once and have Cowork run it automatically — every morning, every Monday, every hour — so work that used to need a calendar reminder just happens.

What a scheduled task actually is

A scheduled task is a Cowork prompt that Claude runs on its own at a cadence you pick — hourly, daily, weekdays, weekly, or manually on demand. Each run opens a fresh Cowork session, does the work, and drops the output wherever you told it to. You don't have to open the task, type the prompt, or remember it's Friday.

The practical picture: on Monday morning you open Claude Desktop and the week's prayer list, inbox digest, and giving summary are all already waiting — Cowork ran them at 6am. You review, edit, and move on to actual ministry.

The one hard requirement

Scheduled tasks only run when your work computer is awake and Claude Desktop is open. If your computer is asleep or the app is closed when a task is scheduled to fire, Cowork skips it — and runs it automatically once the machine wakes back up. Practically, that means scheduled tasks work best for people whose office computer stays on during work hours. If yours sleeps at 5pm on Friday, don't schedule a 6am Monday task and expect it to fire on time; it will run when you arrive Monday morning.

How to create one

There are two ways. Either works.

Method 1 — the /schedule shortcut

  1. Open Cowork and start a new task (or continue an existing one you like).
  2. Write out the prompt you want Claude to run. Test it once live, so you know it produces the kind of output you want.
  3. Type /schedule in the chat input and send.
  4. Claude asks a few follow-up questions with multiple-choice answers — what cadence, what to call the task, when to start. Answer them.
  5. Click Schedule to confirm. The task now appears in the Scheduled section of the left sidebar.

Method 2 — build from scratch

  1. In Cowork, click Scheduled in the left sidebar.
  2. Click New task in the upper right.
  3. Fill in the form: task name, prompt, cadence (hourly / daily / weekdays / weekly / manual), model, working folder.
  4. Click Save.

Use Method 1 when you want Claude to help shape the prompt. Use Method 2 when you already have a polished prompt and just want to schedule it. Once scheduled, all tasks live in that same Scheduled sidebar where you can pause, edit the cadence, run-on-demand, or delete. The official guide is at support.claude.com · Schedule recurring tasks in Claude Cowork.

Rules of thumb for scheduled tasks

  • Test the prompt live once first. Run it as a regular Cowork task and review the output. If it's good, then schedule it. Scheduling a prompt you haven't tested is scheduling a bad output on repeat.
  • Write the guardrails into the prompt. "Skip anything pastoral." "No individual donor names." "Aggregate only." Claude is working alone — you're not there to catch it drifting.
  • Scope to input files that get updated on a known cadence. If the prompt depends on a file that's supposed to be refreshed every Monday but didn't get refreshed this week, Claude should notice and say so rather than making something up.
  • Name output files with the date. Briefing-2026-04-20.docx rather than Briefing.docx. Otherwise the next run overwrites the previous.
  • Review results weekly at first. Just because it ran doesn't mean it ran well. Look at each run's output for the first month — you'll catch subtle prompt issues faster than you'd think.
  • Scheduled tasks consume more usage than chatting. Each run is a fresh Cowork session with file access and multiple steps. Five scheduled daily tasks is not free — if the church account approaches usage limits, scheduled tasks are the first place to audit.

What NOT to schedule at Court Street

Some kinds of work should stay human-initiated even when they're technically recurring. Don't schedule:

  • Anything involving pastoral-crisis or counseling content, even indirectly.
  • Anything that sends a message outside the church — an email to a member, a post on social, a text. Keep output automatic; keep outgoing manual.
  • Anything that makes decisions with money (even just generating draft payment approvals). A human reviews and authorizes every financial action.
  • Anything a member would be surprised to learn was automated. The drafts can be AI; the care has to be visibly human.

That's the second footnote. Paired with Dispatch, scheduled tasks are what turn Cowork from a tool you open when needed into a quiet ministry assistant that's already working when you arrive.

Frequently asked questions

"Is using AI for sermon prep dishonest?"

No more than using a commentary, a study Bible, or a sermon illustration book. Every preacher throughout history has drawn from research tools and other voices. What matters is that the sermon that finally gets preached is yours — your discernment, your voice, your care for the people in front of you. AI is a research assistant. The sermon is yours.

"Will using AI make me a worse writer over time?"

It can, if you use it as a crutch instead of a collaborator. But used intentionally — as a drafting partner you push back on, refine, and often override — it tends to sharpen your writing rather than dull it, because you're editing critically and getting exposure to more approaches. The rule of thumb: if you can't tell what you actually think until you see an AI draft, that's a warning. If an AI draft helps you find what you already think faster, that's a gift.

"What if I'm bad at technology?"

This training assumes no prior AI experience, only basic comfort with email and Word. Claude Desktop's interface is intentionally simple — it's just a chat window and a file-picker. If you hit real friction, talk to the administrator. Many staff members have been surprised at how little technical skill it actually requires once they've installed the app and signed in the first time.

"Can the AI read our member database?"

No, not automatically. Claude has no access to our membership system unless a staff member explicitly uploads or pastes data. Per our policy, named member data with sensitive details (giving amounts, pastoral notes, personal information) doesn't get pasted in at all. The Microsoft 365 connector can see documents, email, and Teams chats — but only the ones you already have permission to see yourself — and it never modifies anything.

"What does Anthropic do with what I type?"

On the licensed organization plan we're using, Anthropic does not use your conversations to train their AI models. Conversations are stored for your use and for operational purposes (reliability, debugging), but they are not used as training data. This is a key reason we chose an organization-tier plan over the free consumer version for church work. Cowork sessions, specifically, are stored locally on your own computer and aren't sent to Anthropic's servers in the same way Chat is.

"What if AI generates something theologically wrong?"

It will, sometimes. This is why every piece of content passes the pastoral and accuracy checks before going out. Treat AI output as a first draft by a seminary intern — often solid, occasionally off, always in need of a knowledgeable review. The more doctrinally sensitive the content, the closer the review.

"Can our AI Usage Policy be updated?"

Yes — annually, or sooner if a new risk or opportunity emerges. Updates go through the pastor and board, and staff are notified. The Never list and Always list are the most stable parts. The technology changes faster than the principles do.

"What if a member asks whether we use AI?"

Tell them the truth. "Yes — we use Claude as a drafting and research assistant for things like newsletters, study guides, and research. Every piece of communication is reviewed and personalized by a staff member before it goes out. We don't use AI for pastoral conversations, counseling, or anything involving sensitive member information." Most members are fine with this, and some are relieved to hear we're being thoughtful.

"What if I realize I made a mistake — pasted something I shouldn't have?"

Contact the administrator that day. We'll walk through what happened, take any cleanup steps available, and document it. Don't hide it. Open handling is the only way to stay safe.

"Do I have to use AI?"

No. If a staff member prefers not to use these tools for some of their work, that's a reasonable choice. The policy is opt-in: available to everyone, required of no one. That said, we'd encourage you to at least complete this training — even if you choose not to use AI day-to-day, it helps to understand what the tools can and can't do when others around you are using them.
